The Road to AdSense (1): Foundations You Build Before Your First Post

24 October 202614 min read2552 words

Most AdSense rejection cases are not rejections of the content itself — they are rejections of everything around it. A site with scattered navigation, slow pages, no legal pages, or no indexing gets rejected even when its articles are good, because the review team does not evaluate a single article: they evaluate whether the "site" as a whole deserves to display Google ads. That is exactly why this series starts where many consider it boring detail: the foundations you build before writing a single word.

In this first part you will walk through seven decisions, ordered the way I actually take them when preparing any new site. None of them requires a big budget, but getting any one of them wrong costs you rework that dwarfs the extra ten minutes it takes to get it right now. At the end of the post there is a final checklist — print it or copy it, and come back to it before your first article.

1) Why foundations first, not content

The fair question: Google says "focus on high-quality content" — so why spend time on domain and structure before writing? The answer is that great content inside a badly built site is like an excellent book with no cover, no table of contents, and no title page. The reader arriving from search does not know where they are, the crawler does not understand how your pages relate, and the AdSense reviewer finds nothing confirming that a real person runs a real publication.

Then there is the rework factor. If you write fifty posts and only then decide to change your URL structure, you will edit fifty posts, their internal links, their sitemaps, and their recorded search entries. Decide the structure beforehand and you edit one configuration. The right order saves you weeks; the wrong order turns "we'll upgrade later" into a someday that never comes.

The rule for the whole series: AdSense does not approve an "article" — it approves a "site". Build the site first.

2) Choosing a niche: three questions, not four

The niche is the subject your site gets professional about. The decision is dangerous because it is the only item on this list that is hard to reverse: you can change hosting in an hour, but you cannot turn a cooking site into a technical one without losing everything you wrote. Before choosing, answer three questions honestly:

First: do you know the subject, or do you know about it? Google's quality guidance states plainly that content should be produced with genuine experience. If everything you know about the topic is the sum of five other articles, you will end up writing a sixth that summarizes them — which is precisely the low-value content quality systems are built to spot, and the most common rejection reason in AdSense reviews today. Choose a topic where you were the user, not just the reader: a tool you mastered, a profession you worked in, a problem you suffered through and solved.

Second: does anyone search for it? Passion with zero search demand means a great site nobody reads. You do not need a paid tool to estimate demand: type the first letters of the topic into Google and watch the autocomplete suggestions, look at the "People also ask" boxes on your results, and browse Arabic communities — specialized Facebook and Telegram groups — noting the questions that repeat and stay without complete answers. Those three places hand you hundreds of genuinely demanded headlines.

Third: do advertisers pay in this field? AdSense sells your ad slots through an auction; topics like insurance, finance, and software attract high bids, others have thin demand. I am not telling you to write about insurance while knowing nothing about it — that violates the first question and ends in failure. The point: if two topics pass the first two questions equally well, pick the one with stronger advertiser interest. One topic that satisfies all three conditions is enough for an entire site. Do not open five sites — Google itself advises concentrating on one site and enriching it rather than spreading yourself across several domains.

3) Domain and hosting: small decisions with long shadows

This is where the first collective mistake happens: hunting for "the best hosting" before deciding what kind of site you are building. The real decision comes first: is your site static (ready-made HTML served as-is) or dynamic (a content system that generates pages on request)?

CriterionStatic site (HTML)WordPress (dynamic)
SpeedExcellent by nature — no database queriesNeeds plugins and tuning to reach acceptable levels
MaintenanceAlmost none — no recurring security updatesWeekly updates for core and plugins
SecurityVery narrow attack surfaceThe most targeted platform worldwide
CostOften free (GitHub Pages and similar)Hosting + backups + security
EditingText files or a simple panelA full dashboard for non-technical owners
Best forSteadily-edited content, owners who learn fastFrequent publishing by non-technical users

There is no universally "correct" option; there is a correct option for you. If you are reading a technical blog, static will likely spare you ninety percent of the performance and security headaches dynamic platforms bring. If your priority is editing from an easy dashboard without touching files, dynamic is legitimate — as long as you commit to the maintenance.

As for the domain, the rules are short and strict: pick a short name that pronounces easily in both Arabic and English, preferably .com or your country's TLD if your audience is strictly local. Avoid long names, multiple hyphens, and numbers that require verbal explanation ("the word one or the digit?"). And do not buy a previously-owned domain without checking its history — an old penalized domain inherits indexing problems that last for months.

If you go dynamic, hosting advice is one line: do not undershoot with an obscure provider to save a couple of dollars. One hour of downtime during an AdSense review equals a rejection, and crowded servers that "stumble occasionally" show up in the crawl record before they show up to you.

4) Page structure: the skeleton before the flesh

Before the first article, your site needs a fixed set of pages — the ones that tell a visitor and the AdSense reviewer alike that behind this content stands a real, reachable, accountable entity:

  • Home: not an empty brochure. The best blog homepage shows recent posts plus one three-line paragraph explaining: this site, for whom, and what the reader gains.
  • About: a page explaining who writes and why that person is credible in this subject. This page is literally one of the things quality reviewers look for when estimating experience (E-E-A-T). Include a way to reach you.
  • Contact: a form or a direct email. A site with no contact route looks like an anonymous storefront, and reviewers notice.
  • Privacy Policy: practically mandatory for AdSense approval; it states what you collect and how you use it.
  • Terms of Use + Disclaimer + Disclosure: together they form the "legal package" recommended before applying, and most superficial rejections trace back to missing them or copying translated templates that have nothing to do with the site.

The rule here is simple: these pages are written once, seriously, and updated whenever something real changes in your site. For a ready checklist of the fourteen points I walk through before any submission, see the full AdSense checklist article.

5) Internal navigation: the three-click rule

Navigation is not decoration in the header; it is the structure through which crawlers read the importance of your pages. Every important page should be reachable within three clicks from the homepage, and every page should answer two questions in seconds: "where am I?" and "where can I go?"

Practically this means three levels, no more: home → category → article. Deep trees (home → section → subsection → article → page 2 of the article) lose visitors and crawlers alike. Give every category a real hub page that describes the topic and links its articles — do not settle for tags that spawn dozens of nearly-empty links, which is among the fastest routes to a "low value content" classification.

Add breadcrumbs at the top of posts: a small line showing "Home ← SEO ← Article Title". It helps readers go back, helps Google understand hierarchy, and sometimes replaces the raw URL in search results. Finally: the main menu shows the few big categories (five to seven), while finer topics live inside articles as links — not crammed into the header like a crowded market.

6) Performance from day one: easiest to start fast

Fixing a slow site is ten times harder than building a fast one, and the slow start indexes slower and measures worse before you even notice. The four starting rules need no deep technical skill:

  1. Hosting/service that is fast for your target geography. If your audience is in Egypt and the Gulf, do not host your single server in South America no matter how attractive the price; with static sites a CDN solves the problem automatically at no cost.
  2. Few fonts, limited weights. One Arabic family + one Latin family, at most three weights combined. Every extra weight nobody uses is extra loading time on a real reader's phone over 3G.
  3. No script in the first paint without a reason. Counters, share buttons, comment widgets — everything loads deferred after content appears, and gets deleted if it serves no real purpose.
  4. Compressed images with known dimensions. Most page weight is images; most image slowness is solved by compressing before upload and setting width and height to prevent layout jumps.

The official metrics Google uses are called Core Web Vitals, and I explain them in depth — with target numbers and fixes — in the detailed Core Web Vitals article. Read it right after this one if technology is not your favorite hobby; the numbers are simpler than you expect.

7) Indexing from day one: do not wait for Google to "find you"

A widespread misconception: "publish and Google will stumble upon you." Reality: a new site with zero external links can spend weeks without a single crawl if you do not invite Google yourself. The first steps need no expertise: create your property in Search Console, submit a sitemap, request inspection of your homepage first, then every important post as you publish during the early weeks.

There is an extra reason not to postpone this, specific to AdSense: the review team sees a well-built site that is crawled and indexed. Presence in Google's index is living proof of a natural site living its life — not a set of pages manufactured to submit an application. I will dedicate an entire series to inspection and the famous exclusion states (like Crawled – currently not indexed), but the initial activation is a day-one responsibility.

8) Why legal pages come before content

A recurring question: "can't I write first and add legal pages a week before applying?" No, for three reasons. First, technical: forms like contact and comments collect data; running them with no privacy policy explaining what happens to that data puts you in a contradictory position — you request a visitor's data and tell them nothing about it. Second, structural: the privacy policy and disclosure reference tools that actually exist (ads later, analytics, forms); writing them early forces conscious thinking about every tool you add afterward. Third, human: a reviewer browsing a site with a complete legal package from day one classifies it as "serious from the start", not "in a hurry to earn".

When you enable ads later, you will add an explicit paragraph about advertising cookies and update the disclosure — the standing rule: every new tool that collects data or displays a third party deserves a same-day update to the legal package.

9) The final checklist before your first post

Ten items; two days of focused work if you are free, or one evening-a-week if you are employed. Compared to the writing weeks ahead, this is the most valuable investment in the entire series.

FAQ

Can I apply for AdSense before thirty posts? You can technically apply with fewer, but approval odds rise clearly with a site that completes its topics and is indexed. There is no official magic number from Google; there is "topical depth" that makes the site look like a source, not an experiment. The realistic rule: apply when every category covers the basics of its subject.

Static or WordPress specifically for the AdSense goal? AdSense does not mandate a technology, but performance and security affect approval and revenue together. Static sites win the speed battle by default; WordPress wins the daily-publishing convenience battle. Choose by your working nature, then commit to optimizing what you chose.

Should I reject low-revenue topics entirely? No. A subject you know deeply with a loyal audience beats a "high-paying" subject you know nothing about in the long run — the first produces original content that builds a site, the second produces rewrites that build a rejection.

When should I change the domain if I discover a naming mistake? Before traffic and indexing accumulate — that is, now. Changing a domain after six months means 301 redirects, re-evaluation waiting, and pain that only a fatal flaw justifies. A good name is enough; a perfect name is a myth.
