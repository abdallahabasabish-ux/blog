An AdSense rejection is not a final verdict on your site — in most cases it is the result of points that could have been checked 24 working hours before submitting. This is the checklist I personally review before submitting any request, ordered by importance, in the same order I use when preparing client sites for approval.

## 1) Original content before quantity

Google does not demand a magic article count — it demands content that adds real value. Ten carefully written articles with personal experience beat fifty copied or "rephrased" ones. Read your own articles and ask: does someone unfamiliar with the topic benefit from this? If the answer is no, the site is not ready yet.

> Practical rule: every article must answer a real question your audience asks — even a simple one.

## 2) The four legal pages

Privacy, Terms, Disclosure, Disclaimer — these are not decoration. The privacy policy must explicitly mention Google's use of cookies upon approval, and describe what data you actually collect. Link them in the footer so any visitor and crawler reaches them from every page.

## 3) Navigation a human and a crawler both understand

Open your site and ask: can a new visitor understand the site's topic in ten seconds? Can they reach any article within three clicks? A clear main menu, logical categories, and internal links between related articles — that is what the review team practically calls "good user experience."

## 4) Domain: top-level is stronger, but subdomains work

An independent domain (example.com) stands stronger than a free subdomain, but blogs on platforms like BlogSpot or custom subdomains do get approved when content is excellent. Do not postpone submission for years waiting for a perfect domain — strengthen the foundation, then apply.

## 5) Indexing first

Before submitting: open Search Console and confirm your key pages are indexed via the "URL Inspection" tool. A site Google has not indexed at all sends a negative signal in an AdSense application. Submit your sitemap.xml and wait at least a week of healthy indexing before requesting.

## 6) Speed and mobile experience

You do not need a 100 PageSpeed score, but a site that opens in seconds on mobile, without screen-covering popups, and with readable text — that is the practical bar. Test your site yourself on a phone using mobile data, not just Wi-Fi.

## 7) No content with policy problems

Review the [official program policies](https://support.google.com/adsense/answer/48182) carefully: no copied content, no misleading information, no restricted topics, and no thin pages dressed with ads. One violating page is enough to reject the whole site.

## 8) Real contact and identity

An "About" page with a real name and a real story, and a working contact email. Anonymous blogs get rejected more — the policies require transparency about the publisher's identity.

## 9) Do not place AdSense (or any other network's) code before approval

Do not add another ad network's code in the spots where ads are expected, and do not fake Google ad placeholders before approval. Keep the site completely clean during review.

## 10) Real images, properly named

Images are either yours or licensed to you — never pulled from Google Images. Give files descriptive names and write descriptive alt text; it helps understanding and indexing together.

## 11) Sitemap + robots.txt in place

Make sure sitemap.xml exists at the site root and is submitted in Search Console, and that robots.txt blocks nothing important. A healthy robots.txt for a blog:

```text
User-agent: *
Allow: /

Sitemap: https://example.com/sitemap.xml
```

## 12) Check every link

A broken link, a missing image, or a button that does nothing — all of these weaken trust. Walk the site page by page and click every link in the menu and footer.

## 13) Submit one site first, from one account

If you own several sites, submit your strongest first. Repeated rejections from one account practically weaken your standing with Google, and a first approval makes adding later sites much easier.

## 14) After submitting: do not change the site during review

Review usually takes days and can reach two weeks in some cases. Big changes during review confuse the outcome or restart the cycle. Wait for the full reply, then work.

## If the request is rejected — what now?

Read the rejection reason in your email carefully (usually generic: "low value content" or "policy violation"). Do not resubmit an hour later with two extra articles — instead: fix the cause in depth, add genuinely original content, wait at least two weeks, then reapply. Most sites pass on the second attempt when the cause is actually fixed, not cosmetically patched.

---

Summary: approval is not a lottery — it is a readiness check. Walk this fourteen-point list honestly and you enter review from the highest possible position; and if you are rejected, you know exactly what to fix.
