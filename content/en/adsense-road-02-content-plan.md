In part one of the series we built the skeleton: niche, domain, fixed pages, navigation, performance, and indexing. Today we stand before the stage that genuinely scares most new site owners: a blank page that is supposed to fill with thirty articles. The number feels huge until you break it into a system — topic clusters, a clear search intent per post, a realistic publishing schedule, and an internal-linking plan that works from day one. This part hands you the full system, then hands you the "do not write" list, because what you avoid at this stage matters as much as what you write.

First, a correction of a common belief: thirty posts is not an official Google number, and no magical article count opens the approval gate. The figure here is a practical norm: thirty posts spread over five or six topic clusters usually suffice to make a site look like a complete source within a mid-size niche, giving the review team — and the reader — a real depth worth navigating. If your niche is narrower, fifteen excellent posts beat thirty shallow ones; the supreme rule always stands above any number in this article.

## 1) The rule governing the whole plan: quality before quantity

Google's high-quality site guidance says, in essence: focus on one site rich in information and authentic in content, do not create multiple pages with duplicate or overlapping material, give users a reason to visit your site and to return, and deliver the information or service you promised. Those four sentences are the test every post in your plan passes before its first character is written.

Practically, run a three-question filter on every post idea before adding it to the schedule:

- **Does it add what has not been said?** If the current top search results already cover the topic fully and you would merely rephrase them, the idea is rejected. A valid idea either covers an uncovered angle, covers the topic deeper with real experience, or addresses a different audience (an Arabic beginner, for example, versus foreign content translated without local context).
- **Can I bring real experience into it?** A "best photo editing tools" post written by someone who collected names from other people's reviews is aggregated content; the same post by someone who used the tools in real work and explains where they failed them is authentic content that builds a site.
- **Does it serve the reader, or only search engines?** A page written to catch a search keyword with no real utility is, by Google's own definition, a page built to deceive — and enough of them poisons the evaluation of the whole site.

With this filter you can now map all thirty ideas without loading your plan with posts that would fail review anyway.

## 2) Topic clusters: how the thirty are distributed

A successful blog is not a flat list of adjacent articles; it is a tree with a trunk and branches. The trunk is the comprehensive pillar article; the branches are detailed posts covering sub-questions, all linked to the trunk and to each other. This order is what makes a reader move from one post to eight inside your site, and what makes Google understand you are an "authority" on the subject rather than a writer of scattered pieces.

The recommended split for thirty: **six clusters × five posts**. Each cluster gets one comprehensive pillar + four detailed posts covering its sub-questions. Here is how the map looks on a hypothetical niche — "earning online":

| Cluster | Pillar post | Detail posts (branches) |
|---|---|---|
| AdSense | The complete AdSense guide for Arab beginners | Preparing your site for approval · Reading revenue reports · Ad placements · Common rejection reasons |
| SEO | SEO fundamentals from zero | Search intent · Keywords · Internal links · Refreshing old posts |
| Web building | Build your first site step by step | Choosing a domain · Hosting · Essential pages · Deployment and custom domains |
| Content | A sustainable writing system | The editorial calendar · Intros that retain · Repurposing content · Bilingual writing |
| Tools | The free content-maker toolbox | Keyword tools · Image tools · Measurement tools · Editing tools |
| Experiments | What I learned from a year of tests | Placement experiment · Publishing rhythm experiment · Old-content refresh · Lessons from rejection |

Notice one important thing: the last cluster — experiments — is the easiest to write and the least contested, because nobody can steal it. Every experiment you lived through is content nobody else owns, and it is precisely what Google means by "added value". Even if your niche is purely technical, dedicate a whole cluster to documenting your tests; you will find they are the most engaging posts and the most capable of ranking.

## 3) Search-intent distribution: three types, each with its own shape

Every search carries an intent, and the biggest failures in Arabic content happen when the post is written with a different intent than the searcher's. In your thirty-post plan, distribute the three types deliberately:

**Informational intent** ("what is", "how does it work", "why") — the decision: the answer sits clearly at the top within the first two paragraphs, then expands beneath. This type suits detail posts, and its on-page time is naturally lower — do not panic about that.

**How-to intent** ("steps", "setup", "detailed guide") — the decision: strict chronological order, numbered steps, screenshots at every critical point, and a common-mistakes list at the end. This type builds reader loyalty more than any other because it accompanies them while they work.

**Comparison/decision intent** ("best", "vs", "or") — the decision: an honest comparison table listing the flaws of every option, plus a conditional recommendation ("choose X if your situation is A"). Comparison posts written with no flaws for the "winner" lose credibility instantly; honesty about weaknesses is what separates a real review from an ad.

A reasonable distribution across the thirty: half informational, a third how-to, a sixth comparisons — roughly matching the actual demand pattern of most technical and monetization niches.

## 4) The headline budget: three formulas suffice

The headline is the only part of your post seen by people who will not read it, yet most owners spend a few random minutes on it. Instead of improvising, adopt three proven formulas and keep them in your template:

1. **The specific promise:** "How to do X without Y" or "X steps to achieve Y" — fits how-to intent; the condition: the number in the headline is literally the number in the post.
2. **The direct question:** "Do you really need X?" or "When do AdSense applications get rejected?" — fits informational intent; the condition: the post answers the question at its start, not its end.
3. **The experience story:** "I tested X for Y months — here is what happened" — fits the experiments cluster; it is the strongest trust-builder and the hardest to imitate.

And beware the fourth formula I mention only to warn against: clickbait headlines promising more than the post delivers. They win one click, then register a bounce and a lost trust — and Google learns from bounce patterns more than you expect.

## 5) The publishing schedule: ten weeks, not one month

Week-one enthusiasm writes five posts, then production stops for a full month. A site that publishes five posts then goes silent looks — to Google and to visitors — like an abandoned project. The steady moderate rhythm always beats intermittent explosions.

| Week | Posts | Running total |
|---|---|---|
| 1-2 | First pillar (AdSense) + two of its branches | 3 |
| 3-4 | SEO pillar + two branches + third AdSense branch | 7 |
| 5-6 | Web-building pillar + two branches + two SEO branches | 11 |
| 7-8 | Content pillar + two branches + two tool branches | 15 |
| 9-10 | Tools pillar + two branches + two experiment branches | 20 |
| 11-14 | Complete the six clusters to thirty (3 posts weekly) | 30 |

Why release clusters gradually rather than all at once? Three reasons: crawling and indexing favor a living site that publishes regularly over a sudden site that dumps a block then sleeps. Every new post needs a bit of maturity from earlier posts to link to them correctly. And third — most important psychologically — the sustainable rhythm stays with you after the thirty, while the first explosion ends in exhaustion.

If your time is tighter, stretch the schedule to twenty weeks at two posts weekly; do not compress below ten weeks unless you genuinely write polished content fast. Publishing twice daily at launch announces to Google a "one-shot content wave" more than a site growing naturally.

## 6) Internal links: a plan executed with every post, not after it

Internal links are the cheapest SEO tool in the world and the most neglected. The practical rule for every new post in the thirty:

- **Two links out of the post:** to its cluster pillar and to one related detail post. This weaves the web and spreads value across your pages.
- **Two links into the post:** after publishing, go back to two older posts connected to the idea and add a natural link to the new one. This always-forgotten step is what turns your new post from an "orphan page" into a natural extension of the tree.
- **Descriptive anchor text, never generic:** "read more" and "click here" waste the link's entire value; use a phrase describing the destination, because anchor texts are among the first signals a crawler uses to understand the target topic.

After the thirty you will have roughly a hundred deliberately woven internal links — this web is exactly what leads a reader of part one of a series to its following parts automatically, and widens a session from one page to four.

## 7) What you must not write in the first thirty

The blacklist matters more than the idea list, and it is extracted directly from the quality checks sites are measured by:

- **"Best of the year" posts with no experience.** Lists someone else collected and you republish under your name — the top low-value classification and the fastest to detect, because aggregated writing patterns are a familiar shape to evaluation systems.
- **Republishing the same idea in different words.** "Top 10 SEO tools", then "SEO tools I recommend", then "the free SEO toolkit" — three headlines for one topic means three pages competing with each other and splitting their value. One idea = one post; genuinely new additions = a new post.
- **"Coming soon" pages or empty shells awaiting content.** Every published page must stand on its own; placeholders count against you immediately.
- **Financial promises backed by numbers you do not own.** "I will show you how I earn $1000 a month" with no report proving it is not just bad faith — it is the seed of cultural rejection for your entire site, and among the fastest ways to expose hollow content. Write what you can evidence, and widen your claims as your evidence grows.
- **Copying your own content between Arabic and English via raw machine translation.** Broken literal translation counts as low-value content in both languages at once. If you will write in two languages, write each audience in clean native-grade language — or postpone the second version until you can sustain its quality.

## 8) Measuring progress: three indicators suffice

Do not open ten dashboards; three weekly indicators are enough to steer your decisions through the thirty-post stage:

1. **Google's index (Search Console):** how many valid pages indexed out of published? If you published ten and only three indexed, the problem is indexing, not writing — pause production and treat it.
2. **Impressions and clicks for target keywords:** each post has one primary target keyword; watch its impressions and clicks weekly. Impressions without clicks = a headline or description problem; no impressions at all = a competition or indexing problem.
3. **Reader behavior on the post page:** do they read past the halfway mark? Do they click an internal link? Free tools are enough to see the general trend, and the trend is enough for the decision: if readers exit at the first heading, the problem is structure, not topic.

What not to do at this stage: compare your two-month-old site to blogs with years of age, and rewrite the plan every week based on one bad stats day. Content needs sixty to ninety days to reveal its true trend; hold the steady rhythm until then.

## 9) Recurring mistakes in the planning stage itself

Before we close, five slips I keep seeing even in well-intentioned content plans:

**A plan with no defined audience.** "My site is about earning online" targets everyone and therefore no one. Define in one line: who is the reader searching for you, and what is their level (a total beginner? a seasoned freelancer?) — then let that line approve every headline and reject the rest.

**Over-widening the clusters.** Six clusters beat twelve thin ones; excessive breadth dilutes depth and gives the "site about everything" impression neither readers nor crawlers like.

**Skipping the pillar post.** Some start with branches because they are "easier", ending with a site of edges and no trunks — losing the cluster's strongest SEO asset: the comprehensive article everything connects to.

**A schedule sized for the ideal person, not the real one.** If you have two hours a day, do not build a plan consuming four. A plan executed at seventy percent, month after month, is a hundred times more valuable than a perfect plan that stops in week three.

**Forgetting that the plan is alive.** After post ten you will learn from your data things you could not know beforehand: topics that rise unexpectedly and others written well but unread. A good plan is adjusted monthly according to real numbers — within its clusters' boundaries, not by opening a new front every month.

## 10) From paper to practice: a weekly routine that runs the plan

A plan that never becomes a weekly routine remains a pretty file. This routine fits a single site owner working evenings — adjust it to your circumstances but keep its three elements: writing, linking, measuring:

**The writing session (three sessions weekly, 90 minutes each).** Each session produces one post from the schedule, in a fixed inner order: headline and intent first (10 minutes), the H2 skeleton (10 minutes), connected writing (50 minutes with no simultaneous editing), then review and language (20 minutes). Forbidding yourself from editing while drafting multiplies real output; editing is another session's job.

**The linking session (15 minutes after each publish).** Publishing does not end at the publish button: two internal links out of the new post, back-links in two older posts, a sitemap update if managed manually, and a URL inspection request in Search Console during the site's early weeks. Fifteen consistent minutes give every post an indexing head start that two weeks of waiting never will.

**The measurement session (20 minutes weekly, on one fixed day).** Review the three indicators: index, keyword appearances, reading behavior. Log them in one simple sheet — four columns: week, published, indexed, note. After eight weeks this sheet becomes your most valuable document, because it turns your decisions from impressions into trends.

## FAQ

**Can I publish all thirty at once if I write them beforehand?**
Writing them in advance is excellent preparation, but do not publish them at once. Release them on a regular schedule for the indexing and behavioral reasons given; a site adding content at a steady pace looks alive, not manufactured in a rush.

**Longer or shorter posts at the beginning?**
Length is a result of depth, not a goal in itself. A detail post that fully answers its question may need fifteen hundred words; a comprehensive pillar may need three thousand. The rule: every post answers its question completely without padding — then it ends.

**Should I translate my posts into English from day one?**
Clean translation widens your audience but doubles the effort. Start Arabic until your rhythm and quality mature, then open the English version with human-grade translation, not raw machine output — broken English hurts both versions.

**How do I guarantee I never repeat topics after the thirty?**
Keep a simple log: for each post, its target keyword and one line on "what it adds over what exists". Before accepting any new idea, search the log; if the "what it adds" answer is weak, the idea either merges into an existing post or gets rejected — the same rule Google applies when evaluating similar pages.
